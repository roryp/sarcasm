# Sarcasm Detection in Speech: Tools and Techniques

## Prosodic Cues and Context in Sarcasm
Sarcasm in spoken language is conveyed through subtle cues in **tone, pitch, and context**. Studies show that sarcastic speech often has a different prosodic profile than sincere speech – for example, English speakers tend to use a **lower average pitch and a flatter intonation** when being sarcastic ([doi:10.1016/j.specom.2007.11.003](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/cheang__pell_2008.pdf#:~:text=A%20reduction%20in%20mean%20F0,signaling%20other%20atti%02tudinal%20and%20affective)). This “deadpan” delivery (reduced pitch variation and a more monotonic tone) can signal that the literal meaning of the words is not sincere ([doi:10.1016/j.specom.2007.11.003](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/cheang__pell_2008.pdf#:~:text=A%20reduction%20in%20mean%20F0,signaling%20other%20atti%02tudinal%20and%20affective)). Other cues include changes in **speech rate** (often slower), intensity, or a slight exaggeration or drawl on certain words ([doi:10.1016/j.specom.2007.11.003](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/cheang__pell_2008.pdf#:~:text=A%20reduction%20in%20mean%20F0,signaling%20other%20atti%02tudinal%20and%20affective)) ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=how%20difficult%20it%20is%20to,assistants%20and%20content%20analysis%20tools)). However, prosodic signals alone are not always consistent; **context** is crucial for interpretation ([doi:10.1016/j.specom.2007.11.003](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/cheang__pell_2008.pdf#:~:text=A%20reduction%20in%20mean%20F0,signaling%20other%20atti%02tudinal%20and%20affective)). The same utterance might be sarcastic in one scenario but sincere in another, so understanding the **context or the words spoken** (and their usual sentiment) greatly helps in detecting sarcasm ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=how%20difficult%20it%20is%20to,assistants%20and%20content%20analysis%20tools)). Even humans rely on both vocal tone and situational context to catch sarcasm, which makes it a challenging task for algorithms (human accuracy for sarcasm is estimated around only ~82% in some studies ([Was that Sarcasm?: A Literature Survey on Sarcasm Detection - arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00425v1#:~:text=Was%20that%20Sarcasm%3F%3A%20A%20Literature,in%20turn%20to%20solve%20it))).

 ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture))Using both text and audio cues (combining **sentiment from words** and **emotional tone from voice**) yields a more complete picture for detecting sarcasm. This multi-modal approach is conceptually illustrated by combining speech waveform features and contextual emoji cues in a sarcasm detection system ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/)). Traditional sarcasm detectors often fell short by looking at a single modality (e.g. just text or just audio), whereas recent methods attempt to **leverage tone, pitch, and context together** for higher accuracy ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture)) ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture)). In practice, detecting sarcasm from voice means extracting relevant **acoustic features** that reflect tone/pitch patterns, and optionally analyzing the **spoken content** or surrounding dialogue.

## Python Libraries for Audio Feature Extraction 
Extracting acoustic features from speech is a key step in building a sarcasm detection model. Several Python libraries can help obtain features related to tone, pitch, and other prosodic elements:

### Librosa
**Librosa** is a popular Python library for general audio analysis. It provides a wide range of features such as **MFCCs (Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients)**, spectral centroids, zero-crossing rate, chroma features, and more ([Feature extraction — librosa 0.11.0rc1 documentation](https://librosa.org/doc/main/feature.html#:~:text=melspectrogram%28)) ([Feature extraction — librosa 0.11.0rc1 documentation](https://librosa.org/doc/main/feature.html#:~:text=Compute%20the%20tonal%20centroid%20features,tonnetz)). Librosa can also perform basic **pitch tracking** (e.g. via `librosa.piptrack`) to estimate fundamental frequency. It’s widely used for music information retrieval but is equally applicable to speech. For sarcasm detection, one could use Librosa to extract features like pitch contours, energy, and spectral features that might differentiate a sarcastic tone. *Ease of use:* Librosa is well-documented and easy to install, making it a convenient choice for feature extraction. However, it does not have built-in speech-specific analytics (it treats audio generally), so identifying the right features (e.g. pitch variance or tempo changes) is up to the developer.

### PyAudioAnalysis
**PyAudioAnalysis** is an audio processing library specifically focused on feature extraction and classification tasks ([Creating Audio Features with PyAudio Analysis - Dolby.io](https://dolby.io/blog/creating-audio-features-with-pyaudio-analysis/#:~:text=Creating%20Audio%20Features%20with%20PyAudio,can%20sift%20through%20the)). It can compute a comprehensive set of **34 short-term features** for each frame of audio (with optional mid-term averaging) ([3. Feature Extraction · tyiannak/pyAudioAnalysis Wiki · GitHub](https://github.com/tyiannak/pyaudioanalysis/wiki/3.-feature-extraction#:~:text=deviation%29%20over%20each%20short,sequence)) ([3. Feature Extraction · tyiannak/pyAudioAnalysis Wiki · GitHub](https://github.com/tyiannak/pyaudioanalysis/wiki/3.-feature-extraction#:~:text=8%20Spectral%20Rolloff%20The%20frequency,of%20the%2012%20chroma%20coefficients)). These features include basic statistics like **zero-crossing rate, energy, spectral entropy**, a bank of 13 MFCCs (and their deltas), and even chroma features ([3. Feature Extraction · tyiannak/pyAudioAnalysis Wiki · GitHub](https://github.com/tyiannak/pyaudioanalysis/wiki/3.-feature-extraction#:~:text=Feature%20ID%20Feature%20Name%20Description,central%20moment%20of%20the%20spectrum)) ([3. Feature Extraction · tyiannak/pyAudioAnalysis Wiki · GitHub](https://github.com/tyiannak/pyaudioanalysis/wiki/3.-feature-extraction#:~:text=8%20Spectral%20Rolloff%20The%20frequency,of%20the%2012%20chroma%20coefficients)). PyAudioAnalysis makes it straightforward to extract a large feature vector from a speech clip and even provides utilities to train classifiers on those features. For example, its built-in functions can read an audio file, slice it into frames (e.g. 50ms), extract all features, and return a feature matrix ([3. Feature Extraction · tyiannak/pyAudioAnalysis Wiki · GitHub](https://github.com/tyiannak/pyaudioanalysis/wiki/3.-feature-extraction#:~:text=from%20pyAudioAnalysis%20import%20ShortTermFeatures%20import,1%5D%29%3B%20plt.show)) ([3. Feature Extraction · tyiannak/pyAudioAnalysis Wiki · GitHub](https://github.com/tyiannak/pyaudioanalysis/wiki/3.-feature-extraction#:~:text=Also%20the%20code%20plots%20the,into%20the%20input%20audio%20recording)). *Capabilities:* While not specialized for prosody, many of the features (e.g. energy, fundamental frequency via MFCCs, etc.) can reflect tone of voice. PyAudioAnalysis also has example code for training an SVM or k-NN on audio features, which could jump-start a sarcasm classifier. *Ease of use:* It’s relatively easy to use for those familiar with Python, though the library’s maintenance is somewhat dated. It’s a good option for quick experimentation or baseline models.

### My-Voice-Analysis and MyProsody
**My-Voice-Analysis** (and the related **MyProsody** library) are Python tools geared towards detailed **voice prosody analysis**. These libraries emphasize features above the phoneme level – essentially capturing the characteristics of speech delivery (suprasegmentals). For instance, MyProsody measures **vocal pitch (fundamental frequency)**, **acoustic intensity**, and **rhythm (speech rate)** ([myprosody | A Python library for measuring the acoustic features of speech (simultaneous speech, high entropy) compared to ones of native speech.](https://shahabks.github.io/myprosody/#:~:text=1,phoneme%20and%20syllable%20duration)). It computes statistics like average syllable pause duration, number of pauses, speaking rate, and numerous pitch-related features (min/max F0, F0 quantiles, jitter, etc.) ([myprosody | A Python library for measuring the acoustic features of speech (simultaneous speech, high entropy) compared to ones of native speech.](https://shahabks.github.io/myprosody/#:~:text=o%20%20%20%20,f0_min)) ([myprosody | A Python library for measuring the acoustic features of speech (simultaneous speech, high entropy) compared to ones of native speech.](https://shahabks.github.io/myprosody/#:~:text=o%20%20%20%20,gender%20recognition)). Such features directly quantify elements of tone and intonation. In the context of sarcasm, one might expect differences in these measures (e.g. a sarcastic utterance might have a lower **f0_index** or a different **intonation_index** due to a flattened pitch contour ([doi:10.1016/j.specom.2007.11.003](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/cheang__pell_2008.pdf#:~:text=A%20reduction%20in%20mean%20F0,signaling%20other%20atti%02tudinal%20and%20affective))). *Capabilities:* My-Voice-Analysis doesn’t require a transcript and focuses purely on audio patterns, which is useful if you want to detect vocal cues of sarcasm without analyzing words ([Voice Analysis and features statistics | by Sabailabo - Medium](https://sabailabo.medium.com/voice-analysis-and-features-statistics-a9020665657b#:~:text=Medium%20sabailabo.medium.com%20%20My,the%20need%20of%20a%20transcription)). It can detect **pitch contours, formants, jitter, shimmer,** and other voice quality metrics that might indicate a “sarcastic” tone. *Ease of use:* These libraries may require a specific Python version (often Python 3.7) and can be a bit finicky to set up ([myprosody | A Python library for measuring the acoustic features of speech (simultaneous speech, high entropy) compared to ones of native speech.](https://shahabks.github.io/myprosody/#:~:text=1,space%20in%20the%20dirctoy%20path)). They are somewhat niche, but they provide high-level functions (for example, computing all prosodic stats in one go). They can be very insightful for research purposes, although the documentation is less extensive than Librosa’s. 

### Parselmouth (Praat Interface)
**Parselmouth** is a Python interface to the Praat software, bringing Praat’s powerful phonetic analysis capabilities into Python ([YannickJadoul/Parselmouth: Praat in Python, the Pythonic ... - GitHub](https://github.com/YannickJadoul/Parselmouth#:~:text=YannickJadoul%2FParselmouth%3A%20Praat%20in%20Python%2C%20the,processing%20and%20phonetic%20algorithms)). With Parselmouth, you can extract precise **pitch tracks, intensity curves, formant frequencies, jitter, shimmer, and Harmonics-to-Noise Ratio (HNR)** – all classic prosodic features ([Detecting Audio Deepfakes Through Acoustic Prosodic Analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14726v1#:~:text=Detecting%20Audio%20Deepfakes%20Through%20Acoustic,Some%20studies)). Essentially, Parselmouth exposes Praat’s algorithms (which are well-validated in speech science) via Python objects. It currently supports Praat objects like **Sound, Spectrogram, Intensity, Pitch, Formants, Harmonicity, and even MFCC computation** ([Introducing Parselmouth: A Python interface to Praat](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2627915/component/file_2627914/content#:~:text=1,way%2C%20a%20user%20can%20access)). For example, one can load an audio file into a `parselmouth.Sound` and call `snd.to_pitch()` or `snd.to_intensity()` to get the pitch and loudness over time ([python - How to calculate audio metrics through parselmouth on a ...](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/57216060/how-to-calculate-audio-metrics-through-parselmouth-on-a-subsequence-of-audio#:~:text=,to_pitch)). *Capabilities:* This library is excellent for fine-grained analysis – you could measure exactly how pitch moves in an utterance and whether a speaker’s pitch range compresses during a sarcastic remark. It also can compute **voice quality features** like jitter (variation in pitch period) or shimmer (variation in amplitude), which might change with a sarcastic or mocking tone. *Ease of use:* Parselmouth is a bit more advanced – it assumes knowledge of Praat’s concepts. Installing it (`pip install praat-parselmouth`) brings in the Praat engine ([Introducing Parselmouth: A Python interface to Praat](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2627915/component/file_2627914/content#:~:text=Parselmouth%20is%20available%20in%20the,The%20accompanying%20docu%02mentation)). Once set up, it’s quite efficient (leverages C++ code of Praat) and saves time compared to running Praat externally. Users comfortable with speech analysis will find it very powerful; newcomers might face a learning curve figuring out which features to extract and how to interpret them. 

**Comparison of Feature Extraction Tools:** For general purposes, **Librosa** and **PyAudioAnalysis** are user-friendly and sufficient to compute standard audio descriptors. Librosa is flexible and well-documented (ideal if you need custom processing or to integrate into deep learning pipelines), whereas PyAudioAnalysis offers a quick all-in-one feature set and even baseline classifiers ([3. Feature Extraction · tyiannak/pyAudioAnalysis Wiki · GitHub](https://github.com/tyiannak/pyaudioanalysis/wiki/3.-feature-extraction#:~:text=deviation%29%20over%20each%20short,sequence)) ([3. Feature Extraction · tyiannak/pyAudioAnalysis Wiki · GitHub](https://github.com/tyiannak/pyaudioanalysis/wiki/3.-feature-extraction#:~:text=Also%20the%20code%20plots%20the,into%20the%20input%20audio%20recording)). On the other hand, **My-Voice-Analysis/MyProsody** and **Parselmouth** target *prosody-specific* measurements – they excel at capturing the nuances of speech delivery (critical for sarcasm, which is largely prosodic). Parselmouth (with Praat) likely provides the **most accurate pitch and formant extraction**, given Praat’s longstanding use in research, but it requires more expertise. MyProsody offers ready-made statistics (e.g., speaking rate, intonation index) that could feed directly into a sarcasm detection model as features, but it’s a smaller project and might not be as actively maintained. In practice, one might use a combination: e.g., **Librosa for spectral features** (MFCCs, etc.) plus **Parselmouth for pitch and jitter**, to cover both general audio characteristics and specific voice tones.

## Machine Learning Approaches to Sarcasm Detection
Once audio features are extracted (and possibly combined with textual transcripts), machine learning models are trained to distinguish sarcastic speech from genuine speech. Approaches range from classic machine learning algorithms to modern deep learning frameworks:

### Classical ML (Feature-Based Models with scikit-learn)
A traditional pipeline for sarcasm detection might involve **feature extraction** followed by a **classifier**. Using a library like scikit-learn, one can train models such as **Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests, or Logistic Regression** on a labeled dataset of sarcastic vs. non-sarcastic utterances. For example, the MUStARD sarcasm dataset’s authors provided a baseline where an SVM is trained on acoustic features ([GitHub - soujanyaporia/MUStARD: Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Dataset](https://github.com/soujanyaporia/MUStARD#:~:text=extract_audio_features)). In this approach, features could include the average pitch, pitch variance, speaking rate, and perhaps textual sentiment or TF-IDF features from the transcript. Scikit-learn makes it easy to experiment with these algorithms – you can quickly do cross-validation to see which features are most informative. *Advantages:* Classical ML models are relatively **easy to interpret** (you can examine feature importances or coefficients to see what cues the model picked up, such as pitch or energy differences). They also work decently with **small datasets**; given that sarcasm datasets are not huge, a well-tuned SVM or ensemble might perform well without needing millions of samples. *Limitations:* These models rely heavily on the quality of the handcrafted features. If sarcasm cues are subtle and non-linear, a simple classifier might not capture them as well as a deep model could. Nonetheless, as a baseline, scikit-learn classifiers offer a **fast development cycle** and often surprisingly strong performance for structured data.

### Deep Learning (Neural Networks with TensorFlow/Keras or PyTorch)
With the advent of deep learning, researchers have explored neural network models to automatically learn complex patterns indicative of sarcasm. Using frameworks like **TensorFlow (Keras)** or PyTorch, one can build models that ingest raw audio signals or high-dimensional feature inputs (and even text transcripts) and output a sarcasm prediction. Common architectures include:
- **Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)** on audio spectrograms: The audio waveform can be converted into a spectrogram or mel-spectrogram image, and a CNN can learn to detect prosodic patterns or voice inflections that correlate with sarcasm (similar to how CNNs recognize patterns in images). 
- **Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)** such as LSTM or GRU networks on time-series features: Here, a sequence of frame-wise features (pitch at each time step, etc.) is fed into an RNN or a bidirectional LSTM, which can model the temporal dynamics of speech (intonation contours, timing, etc.). This can capture how tone evolves across an utterance – e.g., a rise then a sudden fall in pitch might be a cue.
- **Hybrid models (CNN+LSTM or attention mechanisms)**: For instance, a CNN could first extract local features from a spectrogram, and an LSTM could then consider the sequence of these features. Attention layers might be added to let the model focus on particularly informative parts of the audio (e.g., stressed words or pitch extremes).
According to recent work, a wide range of deep architectures have been tried on the sarcasm detection task, including multilayer perceptrons, CNNs, BiLSTMs, combinations like CNN-LSTM, and attention-augmented LSTMs ([Comparison of Deep Learning Models for Automatic Detection of Sarcasm Context on the MUStARD Dataset](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/12/3/666#:~:text=TensorFlow%20and%20Keras%20were%20then,the%20architectures%20that%20were%20employed)). In one comparative study, models were built with **TensorFlow/Keras** using those various architectures to identify sarcasm in the MUStARD dataset ([Comparison of Deep Learning Models for Automatic Detection of Sarcasm Context on the MUStARD Dataset](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/12/3/666#:~:text=TensorFlow%20and%20Keras%20were%20then,the%20architectures%20that%20were%20employed)). The choice of architecture can impact performance: for example, an **attention-based LSTM** might outperform a plain LSTM by focusing on key moments in the speech.

Deep learning also allows **multi-modal** modeling – combining speech and text or other cues in a single network. For example, a model could have an audio sub-network (e.g., an LSTM over acoustic features) and a text sub-network (e.g., a BERT or simple embedding+LSTM for the transcript), which are then merged to make the final decision. The benefit is that the model can learn interactions between what was said and how it was said. Researchers have found that fusing audio, textual, and even **emotion/sentiment features** can boost sarcasm detection performance ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture)) ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=%E2%80%9CWe%20extracted%20acoustic%20parameters%20such,%E2%80%9D)). One approach first does **Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR)** on the audio to get the text, applies **sentiment analysis** to that text, and also extracts acoustic emotion features from the voice; feeding both into a machine learning model improved accuracy in detecting sarcasm ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture)) ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=%E2%80%9CWe%20extracted%20acoustic%20parameters%20such,%E2%80%9D)). Essentially, the network can learn that when **positive words** are spoken with a **disparaging tone** (negative audio emotion), the result is likely sarcasm ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture)).

*Methodologies for Training:* Training a sarcasm detection model typically involves supervised learning. One needs a labeled dataset where each audio (and possibly transcript) is annotated as “sarcastic” or “non-sarcastic.” Given the limited size of most sarcasm corpora, strategies like **cross-validation, data augmentation, and transfer learning** are useful. Data augmentation might involve altering pitch or speed slightly to create variability, though for sarcasm one must be careful not to distort the prosody too much. Some researchers use pre-trained models: e.g., using a pre-trained emotion recognition model on the audio to get features, or using BERT embeddings for text, and then training a top-layer for sarcasm detection ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture)). Frameworks like TensorFlow make it straightforward to incorporate such **pre-trained components** and fine-tune them on sarcasm data. When training deep models, it’s crucial to monitor for overfitting (given small data) – techniques like dropout, regularization, or early stopping are commonly employed. The end result is a model that, given a new speech sample, can output a probability (or binary decision) of whether that sample was sarcastic.

**Choosing Classical vs Deep Learning:** In practice, a good approach is to start simple and then increase complexity. A scikit-learn classifier on a well-chosen set of features can act as a strong baseline. If that isn’t accurate enough, or if you want to capture more nuance, a neural network might be the next step. Deep learning can potentially yield higher accuracy by discovering latent patterns (especially if combined with text), but it requires more effort: collecting enough data, tuning network architectures, and having sufficient computational resources (GPU for training on lots of audio data). Notably, one study reported that even with deep models, performance on the **MUStARD dataset** was in the 70-80% F1-score range ([[PDF] Enhancing Multimodal Sarcasm Detection with Context-Aware Self ...](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.224.pdf#:~:text=,1%2C%20surpassing%20the)) – indicating this is a tough problem and even advanced models are not perfect yet. The best results often come from **hybrid approaches** that intelligently combine **acoustic features, lexical cues, and context** through machine learning ([Improving sarcasm detection from speech and text through attention ...](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/poma/article/54/1/060002/3305267/Improving-sarcasm-detection-from-speech-and-text#:~:text=Improving%20sarcasm%20detection%20from%20speech,data%20to%20enhance%20sarcasm%20detection)).

## APIs and Cloud Services for Sarcasm-Related Analysis
While there is no off-the-shelf “sarcasm detector” API as of yet, several cloud services and APIs provide building blocks that could be used to infer sarcasm, or at least the emotional tone of speech. Here we consider Affectiva’s Emotion API and Google’s speech and language APIs:

### Affectiva’s Emotion Recognition API (Speech)
Affectiva, known for its facial expression analysis, also developed an **emotion recognition API for speech**. This service analyzes an audio clip of speech and returns probabilities for a set of emotional states. Initially, Affectiva’s speech emotion model focused on the **six basic emotions** (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise) in vocal tone ([Introducing Affectiva’s Emotion Recognition through Speech](https://blog.affectiva.com/introducing-affectivas-emotion-recognition-through-speech#:~:text=,Emotion%20AI)). It can also detect things like laughter, hesitation sounds (“um”, “uh”), and other paralinguistic signals ([Introducing Affectiva’s Emotion Recognition through Speech](https://blog.affectiva.com/introducing-affectivas-emotion-recognition-through-speech#:~:text=understanding%20you%3F%E2%80%9D)). The idea is to capture how someone’s voice indicates their mood or attitude. However, **sarcasm is not a basic emotion** – it’s more of a complex communicative intent. The Affectiva API might not explicitly output “sarcasm” as a category. That said, it could provide clues: for example, it might detect a mismatch where the lexical content seems positive but the tone sounds negative or flat. In theory, one could use Affectiva’s output (say it reports moderate **anger or sadness** in the tone while the words are positive) as a heuristic for sarcasm. Affectiva has mentioned combining modalities (face + voice) for better emotion AI ([Introducing Affectiva’s Emotion Recognition through Speech](https://blog.affectiva.com/introducing-affectivas-emotion-recognition-through-speech#:~:text=According%20to%20the%207,using%20both%20face%20and%20voice)), which aligns with the idea that sarcasm might be caught when voice and context are considered together. *Ease of use:* Affectiva’s API is commercial – you send audio and get back emotion metrics, which is straightforward for developers. It abstracts away the signal processing and machine learning. *Accuracy:* For clear cases of emotion, Affectiva is quite good, but for sarcasm its effectiveness is unproven. Sarcasm often involves a **subtle or controlled emotional tone** (e.g. a deliberately calm voice when the content implies you should be excited). Such subtleties might not trigger strong emotion readings. Therefore, while Affectiva can enrich a system (with emotional features like “tone sounds annoyed”), it likely would need to be part of a larger sarcasm logic rather than a standalone solution.

### Google Cloud Speech-to-Text (and NLP)
**Google Cloud Speech-to-Text (STT)** is a widely-used API to transcribe spoken audio to text. By itself, STT does not detect sarcasm or even emotion – it focuses on converting audio to the correct words. It can handle nuances like punctuation or sentence boundaries to some extent, but it won’t label something as sarcastic. However, the transcription it produces can be fed into other analyses. Google offers a **Natural Language API** that can do **sentiment analysis on text** ([Google Cloud Speech AI Guide](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/your-ultimate-guide-to-speech-on-google-cloud#:~:text=Google%20Cloud%20Speech%20AI%20Guide,transcript%20is%20expressing%20negative)). One could combine these: first get the transcript via Speech-to-Text, then analyze the sentiment of the text. If the text’s sentiment is very positive but the situation or perhaps an external emotion reading of the voice is negative, that conflict might indicate sarcasm. In fact, Google’s documentation suggests pairing STT with their NLP sentiment to figure out if an *audio* expresses positive or negative sentiment overall ([Google Cloud Speech AI Guide](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/your-ultimate-guide-to-speech-on-google-cloud#:~:text=Combined%20with%20the%20STT%20API%2C,transcript%20is%20expressing%20negative)). The catch is that sarcasm often **flips the intended sentiment**: the transcript might read as positive (e.g. “Great job, really impressive”) while the true intent is negative. A naive sentiment analysis would be fooled (labeling it positive). So, Google’s APIs out-of-the-box would likely misclassify sarcastic statements if used alone. *Potential use:* Developers can use Google STT to get high-quality transcripts (which is very helpful because understanding the verbal context is half the battle in sarcasm). Then, a custom model or rule could be applied that considers audio features. Google does not currently provide an emotion-from-voice API in their cloud offerings (as of writing), though third-party solutions or AutoML could be built on their platform. Another angle is using **Google’s Dialogflow** or conversational AI, which can maintain context. But again, recognizing sarcasm isn’t a built-in feature. Overall, **Google’s speech API is extremely useful for the “context” part** (the words), and with the transcript one could use any number of NLP sarcasm detectors (there are pre-trained transformers for sarcasm in text, etc.). For the acoustic part, one might need to use open-source tools (like we discussed) or other services, because Google’s speech API doesn’t give prosodic features directly. 

### Other Related APIs and Tools
Beyond Affectiva and Google, a few other services merit note: **IBM Watson Tone Analyzer** can analyze text for tones (like joy, anger, or sarcasm) – interestingly, Watson’s NLU had a category for tentative or **“humor/sarcasm” tone in text** in some iterations ([Emotion detection of social data: APIs comparative study - PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10172785/#:~:text=Emotion%20detection%20of%20social%20data%3A,to%20send%20the%20request)). That’s text-based though, not from voice directly. **Microsoft Azure Cognitive Services** have speech sentiment analysis in their platform (e.g., the Speech SDK can do emotion detection on voice to some extent). Those typically classify into broad emotions or energy levels, rather than sarcasm explicitly. Another company, **Beyond Verbal (now Vocalis Health)**, used to offer an API to detect emotions and attitudes from voice; again, sarcasm per se was not a direct output, but it could flag “negative sentiment voice” or “complex tone.” In summary, current APIs excel at *what they are trained for* – basic emotions, sentiment, or transcription – but sarcasm is a higher-level inference requiring a mix of cues. So, a developer might use these APIs as **components**: e.g., use a speech emotion API to get tone features, use a speech-to-text API to get content, and then feed both into a custom sarcasm detection model or heuristic.

## Datasets and Research Resources for Sarcasm in Speech
Research into sarcasm detection has produced some useful datasets and open-source resources, though it’s a relatively nascent field compared to text-based sarcasm detection. Here are notable examples:

- **MUStARD (Multimodal Sarcasm Dataset)**: Introduced by Castro et al. (ACL 2019), MUStARD is a corpus of **345 sarcastic and 345 non-sarcastic video clips** taken from TV shows ([GitHub - soujanyaporia/MUStARD: Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Dataset](https://github.com/soujanyaporia/MUStARD#:~:text=Towards%20Multimodal%20Sarcasm%20Detection%20,Obviously%20Perfect%20Paper)). Each clip comes with the *audio* (dialogue of the utterance), the *transcript*, and even the *dialogue context* (preceding lines) ([GitHub - soujanyaporia/MUStARD: Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Dataset](https://github.com/soujanyaporia/MUStARD#:~:text=automated%20sarcasm%20discovery,the%20scenario%20where%20it%20occurs)) ([GitHub - soujanyaporia/MUStARD: Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Dataset](https://github.com/soujanyaporia/MUStARD#:~:text=identifier%20%28e.g.%2C%20,dictionary%20of%20the%20following%20items)). This dataset was specifically created for sarcasm research and is multimodal (it includes visual, but one can use just the audio and text). For example, an utterance like *“It’s just a privilege to watch your mind at work.”* (said by Sheldon in *The Big Bang Theory*) is labeled sarcastic, and the dataset provides the prior lines that set the context ([GitHub - soujanyaporia/MUStARD: Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Dataset](https://github.com/soujanyaporia/MUStARD#:~:text=%7B%20,)) ([GitHub - soujanyaporia/MUStARD: Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Dataset](https://github.com/soujanyaporia/MUStARD#:~:text=,)). MUStARD is open-source and comes with baseline code – the authors provided scripts to extract audio features and train an SVM classifier ([GitHub - soujanyaporia/MUStARD: Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Dataset](https://github.com/soujanyaporia/MUStARD#:~:text=extract_audio_features)). It’s a valuable resource for training and benchmarking models, though relatively small in size. There is also **MUStARD++** (an expanded version with about 691 sarcastic samples) annotated with emotion labels and additional non-sarcastic examples, which aims to balance and provide more data ([Benchmarking and Expansion to Improve Multimodal Sarcasm ...](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01430#:~:text=Benchmarking%20and%20Expansion%20to%20Improve,modal%20phenomenon)). These datasets highlight the importance of multi-modal cues: using just the transcript from MUStARD, or just audio, is challenging – combining them yields better results in research.

- **Open-Source Projects**: Aside from the MUStARD repository ([GitHub - soujanyaporia/MUStARD: Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Dataset](https://github.com/soujanyaporia/MUStARD#:~:text=extract_audio_features)), which contains data and baseline models, other open-source contributions include implementation examples on GitHub or Kaggle. For instance, some researchers have shared code for **multi-modal sarcasm detectors** that use attention mechanisms to fuse audio and text ([[PDF] Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Based on MUStARD Dataset](https://monagandhi09.github.io/asset/pdf/SarcasmDetector.pdf#:~:text=,on%20the%20MUStARD%20dataset%2C)). There was a term project (Monica Gandhi et al.) that used an attention-based LSTM on MUStARD and reported improved accuracy with such architectures ([[PDF] Multimodal Sarcasm Detection Based on MUStARD Dataset](https://monagandhi09.github.io/asset/pdf/SarcasmDetector.pdf#:~:text=,on%20the%20MUStARD%20dataset%2C)). While many public projects focus on sarcasm in text (e.g., detecting sarcasm in Twitter or headlines), a few address spoken sarcasm. A press release by University of Groningen (Gao *et al.* 2024) describes a system that combines speech and emoji context; the accompanying media suggests they achieved around **75% accuracy** using their multi-aspect audio analysis approach ([AI Can Detect Sarcasm With Help From 'Friends'?—Yeah, Right!](https://decrypt.co/231125/sarcasm-detector-ai-trained-on-tv-research-paper#:~:text=AI%20Can%20Detect%20Sarcasm%20With,accuracy)). This indicates current open systems are in that performance ballpark.

- **Other Datasets**: Beyond MUStARD, a **Spanish Sarcasm Corpus** (Aníjar and Hämäläinen, 2021) was compiled with some sarcastic and non-sarcastic clips from Spanish TV shows ([A Survey of Multimodal Sarcasm Detection](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0887.pdf#:~:text=Prompting%20and%20LLMs%20Ding%20et,the%20ability%20of%20multimodal%20LLMs)) ([A Survey of Multimodal Sarcasm Detection](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0887.pdf#:~:text=The%20methods%20discussed%20thus%20far,Wang%20et)). It’s smaller but shows interest in multilingual sarcasm. There have also been **audio + textual sarcasm** shared tasks (e.g., some competition tasks that include conversational context – however, many of those focus on text conversation context ([Shared Task on Sarcasm Detection - CodaLab - Competition](https://competitions.codalab.org/competitions/22247#:~:text=Competition%20competitions,2018))). While no huge multimodal sarcasm dataset like those in image-text exists, these curated datasets provide crucial training/evaluation material. Researchers sometimes also augment data by using **acted sarcasm** (asking people to say phrases sarcastically vs normally) to get more examples, though acted data can differ from natural sarcasm.

- **Recent Research Papers**: Sarcasm detection in speech has been explored in venues like Interspeech, ACL, and specialized workshops. A recurring theme is the use of **attention and multimodal fusion**. One paper proposed an approach that **“synergizes audio, textual, sentiment, and emotion data”** to improve sarcasm detection ([Improving sarcasm detection from speech and text through attention ...](https://pubs.aip.org/asa/poma/article/54/1/060002/3305267/Improving-sarcasm-detection-from-speech-and-text#:~:text=Improving%20sarcasm%20detection%20from%20speech,data%20to%20enhance%20sarcasm%20detection)) – essentially stacking various inputs to catch the discrepancy between them. Another line of research looks at **acoustic-prosodic features** alone: e.g., examining specific pitch patterns or the timing of speech segments when sarcastic. Cheang and Pell’s classic study (2008) analyzed acoustic differences in sarcastic speech (finding the pitch flattening result we discussed) ([doi:10.1016/j.specom.2007.11.003](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/cheang__pell_2008.pdf#:~:text=A%20reduction%20in%20mean%20F0,signaling%20other%20atti%02tudinal%20and%20affective)), which informs modern feature design. A more computational work by Tepperman et al. (2006) attempted a sarcasm recognizer on acted speech (“yeah right” phrases) using prosody and achieved moderate success, indicating that prosody has signal but not 100% reliability. In 2021-2023, with deep learning, we see papers using **transformers** and even **pre-trained multimodal models** (like using BERT for text and CNN for audio, then combining). For example, an **IJCAI 2023 survey** on multimodal sarcasm detection notes that fusing BERT (text) and ResNet (video) features with graphs or cross-attention yields the best results so far ([A Survey of Multimodal Sarcasm Detection](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0887.pdf#:~:text=to%20a%20common%20feature%20space,only%20encoder%2C%20to)) ([A Survey of Multimodal Sarcasm Detection](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0887.pdf#:~:text=achieving%20good%20performance,image%20encoding%20and%20an%20inter)). Although those works include video, the audio component remains key – the voice carries the sarcasm in many cases.

In summary, the research community is actively exploring how to reliably detect sarcasm from voice. Datasets like MUStARD provide a testbed, and a combination of **carefully engineered features** and **learned representations** seems to work best. As an end-user or developer, you can leverage these findings by using the available data to train your model and validate it. If you’re building an application, it might be beneficial to incorporate an existing dataset for a particular language or domain to bootstrap your model’s understanding of sarcastic cues.

## Comparative Analysis and Practical Considerations
Bringing together the tools and techniques:

- **Feature Extraction Tools:** Librosa and PyAudioAnalysis are easy to use and integrate well with Python ML workflows (one-line commands to get dozens of features). They are a great starting point for implementing sarcasm detection because you can quickly get numerical descriptors of the audio. My-Voice-Analysis/MyProsody and Parselmouth, while more specialized, offer deeper insight into prosody which is exactly where sarcasm lives (tone, pitch, etc.). In terms of *ease of use*, Librosa probably wins (a single `pip install librosa` and excellent docs). Parselmouth might rank highest in *accuracy of feature measurement* (since it uses Praat’s robust algorithms ([Introducing Parselmouth: A Python interface to Praat](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2627915/component/file_2627914/content#:~:text=1,way%2C%20a%20user%20can%20access))), which can be important if subtle differences in pitch or jitter are predictive. If a user has no background in speech processing, starting with Librosa or PyAudioAnalysis for broad features is wise, then perhaps adding Parselmouth for a few key prosodic measures (like mean pitch, pitch variance, speaking rate) to enrich the feature set.

- **Machine Learning Approaches:** For a quick solution, a **scikit-learn classifier** (SVM/RandomForest) on extracted features is attractive – it’s straightforward to implement and fast to train. This might yield a decent accuracy (perhaps detecting obvious sarcasm instances where prosody is very distinct). However, for higher accuracy and capturing more complex patterns, **deep learning** with TensorFlow (or PyTorch) provides flexibility. You could experiment with CNNs on spectrograms or LSTMs on sequences; these models might uncover patterns like “intonation falls at the end in a certain way” that are hard to express in a few handcrafted features. They also allow combining modalities (audio + text) seamlessly. The trade-off is that deep models need more data and tuning – with small datasets, you risk overfitting or capturing speaker-specific traits rather than general sarcasm indicators. From a *practical implementation* view: if you have a limited dataset, you might actually get more mileage from a feature-based approach (perhaps using data augmentation and an ensemble classifier) than a huge deep network. If you have time and resources, implementing a multimodal deep model and possibly leveraging **pre-trained models** (like using a pretrained emotion recognition network on the audio, or a language model on text) will likely push performance further. Frameworks like Keras make building prototypes relatively accessible, but you still need to do careful validation.

- **APIs vs Custom Models:** Relying solely on existing APIs (like Affectiva or cloud services) won’t give you a direct sarcasm detector, but they can accelerate development of components. *Ease of use:* APIs are extremely easy to call and integrate; for instance, with a few REST calls you can get an emotion timeline for a voice clip from Affectiva or a transcript from Google. If your application needs a quick judgement and you’re okay with indirect inference, you might combine an API’s output with some logic (e.g., if Affectiva says the speaker’s tone is “anger” but the words are positive, flag as sarcasm). However, *accuracy* for sarcasm using such indirect means will not match a model explicitly trained for sarcasm. They may miss a lot of subtle sarcastic remarks or raise false alarms when someone’s tone is flat for other reasons. Custom-trained models, built on relevant data, currently show better accuracy for this specific task (with F1-scores in the mid-70s on benchmark datasets when both audio and text are used). In practice, one could use APIs to **augment data** – for example, use an emotion API to label each training example with an emotion vector as additional features for your model. Google’s STT is almost a necessity if you want to include textual context, since manual transcription is not scalable; its accuracy in transcribing words is top-notch, ensuring your model isn’t misled by transcript errors. But beyond that, a custom approach is needed to truly detect sarcasm.

- **Context and Multimodality:** A key takeaway from recent research is that **no single feature or modality is sufficient** for high accuracy. Pure audio-based sarcasm detection might catch the obvious cases (the drippingly sarcastic tone), but miss those where the speaker’s tone is subtle and only the context makes it clear. Conversely, text alone often fails because sarcasm text can appear benign or even overly polite. The best performing systems use **multiple features:** acoustic prosody features + lexical features (like the presence of hyperbolic words or known sarcasm cues in text) + contextual features (previous sentences, speaker identity, etc.). Incorporating these in a machine learning model (e.g., through feature fusion or multi-input neural nets) is a bit more complex, but practically it yields more robust detection ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture)) ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=%E2%80%9CWe%20extracted%20acoustic%20parameters%20such,%E2%80%9D)). For an implementation, this means you might pipeline several components: audio feature extraction (tone, pitch, etc.), speech-to-text for content, maybe a sentiment analysis or emotion detection on either modality, and then a classifier that considers all of these signals.

- **Practical Implementation Tips:** If one were to build an end-to-end sarcasm detection system:
  1. **Gather Data:** Use datasets like MUStARD to train, but also consider augmenting with your own collected samples (e.g., record some sarcastic/non-sarcastic examples) to fine-tune the model to your domain (since sarcasm on TV might differ from sarcasm in customer service calls, etc.).
  2. **Feature Engineering:** Start with proven prosodic features: mean pitch, pitch range, pitch variance, speech rate (words per second or syllable length), loudness, and perhaps spectral features indicating vocal effort. Also include textual features: e.g., sentiment of the utterance, presence of intensifiers or hedges, etc. Libraries like Librosa/Parselmouth and NLTK or TextBlob for sentiment can help here.
  3. **Model Training:** Implement a simple classifier with scikit-learn to set a baseline accuracy. Then incrementally try more complex models. For deep learning, you could use Keras to build a model that takes two inputs (audio feature sequence + text sequence). Ensure you set aside some data for testing, as it’s easy to overfit given the small data.
  4. **Evaluation:** Evaluate not just overall accuracy but where it fails. You may find, for example, that the model catches long, drawn-out sarcastic remarks but misses very short quips, or vice versa. This can guide you to add features or adjust the model (maybe the model needs to consider dialogue context of preceding lines, which you can feed in if available).
  5. **Real-time Considerations:** If deploying in a real-time system (say a voice assistant that detects sarcasm), consider the latency of feature extraction and inference. Libraries like Librosa are fast enough for real-time feature extraction on short utterances. A small neural network can run in real-time on modern hardware. Cloud APIs will introduce latency (network calls), so for real-time it might be better to use on-device models once they are trained.
  
- **Accuracy Expectations:** As of recent research, even sophisticated models hover around 70–80% accuracy on balanced datasets ([[PDF] Enhancing Multimodal Sarcasm Detection with Context-Aware Self ...](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.224.pdf#:~:text=,1%2C%20surpassing%20the)). This implies sarcasm detection is far from solved. In practical terms, your system might be a complement to user experience rather than a definitive judge – e.g., a conversational agent might use a detected sarcasm cue to respond with, “I sense you might be joking,” rather than completely changing its behavior. It’s good to be aware that a sarcasm detector will have false positives and negatives. Tuning the threshold (if using a probability output) can adjust how sensitive it is. Depending on the application, you might prefer to err on the side of not accusing someone of sarcasm unless fairly sure.

**Conclusion:** Detecting sarcasm in voice is an interdisciplinary challenge, at the intersection of signal processing, linguistics, and machine learning. By leveraging tools like Librosa and Parselmouth to capture tone/pitch features, and frameworks like scikit-learn or TensorFlow to train models, one can build a pipeline to tackle the problem. Incorporating context through transcripts (via Google STT or similar) and even emotion APIs can enrich the feature set, aligning with what recent research suggests (that multi-cue approaches work best ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=Traditional%20sarcasm%20detection%20algorithms%20often,for%20a%20more%20complete%20picture)) ([Building a Better Sarcasm Detector #ASA186](https://acoustics.org/building-a-better-sarcasm-detector-asa186/#:~:text=%E2%80%9CWe%20extracted%20acoustic%20parameters%20such,%E2%80%9D))). In comparing the tools: simpler libraries and models offer quick insights and are easier to implement, while more complex ones promise higher accuracy at the cost of development effort. As open-source datasets (e.g. MUStARD) and research findings continue to grow, we can expect the accuracy to improve. For now, a **comparative strategy** – using a mix of the mentioned libraries for feature extraction, and trying both classical and deep learning methods – is the optimal way to determine what works best for a given sarcasm detection task. Each tool has its strengths: *Librosa* for versatility, *PyAudioAnalysis* for convenience, *My-Voice-Analysis/Parselmouth* for prosodic depth, *scikit-learn* for speed and simplicity, *TensorFlow* for modeling power, and *APIs* for quick supplementary signals. By thoughtfully combining these, one can create a sarcasm detection system that is both practical and reasonably accurate given the current state of technology.
